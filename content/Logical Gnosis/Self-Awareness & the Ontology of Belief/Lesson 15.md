___
## Can a State Be Observed Without Becoming an Identity?

___
## Lesson 15

We have spent the previous lessons learning to observe what happens in experience.

A thought can appear.

It can be noticed.

It can be followed.

Or it can be left without continuation.

A configuration can begin to form around a thought,

a memory,

a sensation,

an expectation,

or a direction of attention.

Now we take the next step.

Not simply to observe a state.

Not simply to avoid identifying with it.

But to examine whether a state can become something more precise:

**an object of deliberate examination.**

If you are angry, you usually experience anger from within anger.

If you are anxious, you usually experience anxiety from within anxiety.

The state does not merely contain thoughts and sensations.

It also influences what becomes important,

what seems threatening,

which interpretations appear convincing,

and which actions seem immediately necessary.

The state therefore behaves like a configuration.

And this is what we need to investigate.

Not:

**How can I stop feeling this?**

But:

**Can I see the configuration itself?**

And if I can see it, can I examine its structure without having to obey it?

That is today's experiment.

Wait for a relatively ordinary state to appear.

Irritation.

Unease.

Frustration.

Anxiety.

Impatience.

Excitement.

Defensiveness.

Do not create one deliberately.

Use something that actually happens.

Stay with it for a moment and notice what is present.

Perhaps a thought keeps returning.

Perhaps a memory has become unusually available.

Perhaps the body has become tense.

Perhaps attention keeps returning to one object.

Perhaps you are repeatedly imagining what might happen next.

Perhaps one interpretation has become much more convincing than the alternatives.

Do not explain the state yet.

Just examine it.

You may find several elements:

a thought,

a memory,

a sensation,

an expectation,

a direction of attention,

an emotional tone,

an impulse toward action.

The purpose is not to produce a perfect psychological analysis.

It is to recognize something simpler:

**the state has structure.**

It is not an indivisible block.

It is a configuration.

Now notice how the state normally presents itself.

If you are irritated:

_I am irritated._

If you are anxious:

_I am anxious._

These are ordinary descriptions.

But when a state becomes strong, the description can gradually become a statement about identity:

_I am an anxious person._

_I am an angry person._

_I always react this way._

The state is no longer merely something occurring.

It has become part of an explanation of what you are.

Instead, try:

**Anger is present.**

**Anxiety is present.**

**A state of frustration is present.**

Do not use these sentences as affirmations.

The point is not to convince yourself that the state is not yours.

The point is to change the functional position

from which you examine it.

The state is present.

The thought is present.

The bodily sensation is present.

The impulse is present.

But the state has now been designated as something that can be looked at, examined, and compared.

That is the first structural move.

Now examine the direction of the state.

Suppose irritation is present.

What does it seem to require?

_Answer immediately._

_Defend yourself._

_Explain what happened._

_Prove that the other person is wrong._

_Continue thinking about what they said._

Perhaps anxiety says:

_Check again._

_Prepare more._

_Imagine what could go wrong._

_Do something immediately._

The state does not merely contain an emotion.

It presents a direction.

It makes certain continuations more salient than others.

But a state can generate a direction without that direction becoming an obligation.

The presence of an impulse does not establish that the impulse must be followed.

The presence of a thought does not establish that the thought must be continued.

The presence of an emotional configuration does not establish that it determines what happens next.

Once the direction becomes visible, it can be examined.

Now look at the configuration more closely.

Imagine that the state is a construction placed in front of you.

Not because it is unreal.

Quite the opposite.

Because it is real enough to be examined.

The thought is one element.

The memory is another.

The bodily tension is another.

The expectation is another.

The interpretation is another.

The impulse is another.

They have been operating together. That is why they have appeared as one state.

But operating together does not mean that they are inseparable.

**A configuration can be experienced as a whole while still being composed of distinguishable functions.**

This is more precise than simply saying:

“observe your emotions.”

We are beginning to treat the state as something that can be worked with.

Look first at **Recall**.

The state may be using material that already exists:

a previous conversation,

a remembered failure,

a familiar image,

a previous emotional reaction,

a sentence someone said months ago,

or a prediction based on something that happened before.

Ask:

**What prior material is this state making available?**

The present state may therefore be assembling the present together with selected material from the past.

That material has become available because it was previously formed.

This is Recall.

And it reveals an important possibility:

**a current configuration may depend partly on organization formed earlier.**

The same system that retrieves material associated with anger

may also be capable of retrieving other previously formed organizations.

This does not mean that memory is under unlimited voluntary control.

It means something more precise:

**Previously formed organization can become available for re-entry into a new configuration.**

We do not yet need to deliberately perform that operation.

For now, simply recognize that previously formed organization can become available again.

Now look at **Differentiation**.

Where is attention going?

What has become foreground?

What has disappeared into the background?

If you are irritated with someone,

perhaps every new detail confirms the irritation.

If you are anxious,

perhaps every ambiguous signal begins to look significant.

If you are afraid,

possible threats become unusually prominent.

The state is therefore not merely carrying content.

It is organizing relevance.

It is differentiating the field.

Some things become important.

Others become almost invisible.

Now notice something outside the current direction.

Not to suppress the state.

Not to deny it.

Simply to test whether the field can be differentiated differently.

If attention has narrowed toward one thought,

notice another available element.

If it is fixed on one interpretation,

notice another interpretation that is actually present.

If the body has become the entire focus,

notice the wider field of sensation.

You are testing whether the current configuration is the only organization available within the field.

Now make the experiment more explicit.

Suppose the same memory were present,

but the interpretation were different.

What would happen?

Suppose the same situation were present,

but attention were directed differently.

What would happen?

Suppose the same bodily sensation were present,

but it were not interpreted as evidence of danger.

What would happen?

Suppose the same thought appeared,

but another thought were deliberately given greater attention.

What would happen?

The purpose is not to answer these questions abstractly.

It is to discover whether changing one functional relation changes the configuration as a whole.

This is the beginning of **reconfiguration**.

The state is no longer merely something happening to you.

Its organization has become visible.

And once organization becomes visible,

different organizations become conceivable.

This is the central discovery of the lesson.

You have not learned how to control every state.

You have not learned how to eliminate unpleasant emotions.

You have not established that every state can be changed immediately.

You have discovered something more fundamental:

**A state is a configuration.**

It contains elements.

Those elements have relations.

Those relations create a particular organization.

That organization creates a particular direction of attention,

a particular range of interpretations,

a particular set of impulses,

and a particular sense of what should happen next.

Because the organization can be distinguished,

it can become an object of deliberate examination.

That changes your relationship with it.

You are no longer forced to encounter the state only as:

**“this is happening to me.”**

You can also encounter it as:

**“this is a configuration currently operating.”**

That is not emotional detachment.

It is structural visibility.

And structural visibility is a condition for deliberate intervention.

This also clarifies the question of identity.

When all elements of a state operate together, the configuration can become transparent.

You no longer see the organization.

You simply experience its conclusion:

_I am angry._

_I am anxious._

_I am a failure._

_I am this kind of person._

But once the configuration becomes visible,

the identity claim can itself become another element.

The state is present.

Then the state is interpreted.

Then the interpretation can become a statement about the self.

These are different operations.

And because they are different, they can be examined separately.

This does not mean that all self-reference is false.

It means something narrower:

**A state does not automatically acquire the status of identity merely because it is present.**

Now examine the state once more.

Do not suppress it.

Do not dramatize it.

Do not justify it.

Do not replace it with positive thinking.

Keep it visible.

Notice its elements.

Notice its direction.

Notice what it recruits.

Notice what it excludes.

Notice what it seems to demand.

Then ask:

**If this were a construction, what could be changed?**

Perhaps less attention to one thought.

Perhaps less rehearsal of one memory.

Perhaps a different interpretation.

Perhaps a different direction of attention.

Perhaps a different bodily organization.

Perhaps a different anticipated outcome.

Perhaps a different action.

Or perhaps you discover that you do not yet know.

That is also useful.

The purpose is not to produce a successful modification.

It is to move the state from **implicit experience** into **explicit examination**.

This also clarifies the difference between suppression and reconfiguration.

Suppression says:

**I do not want this state to exist.**

Reconfiguration says:

**I can see how this state is organized. What happens if I alter its organization?**

Suppression fights the existence of the configuration.

Reconfiguration investigates its structure.

The question is therefore not whether another configuration feels pleasant.

Calm is one possible configuration.

Clarity is another.

Patience is another.

Firmness is another.

Determination is another.

The point is not to impose one preferred emotional state on every situation.

The point is to discover that the state currently occupying the field

is not necessarily the only configuration available.

This is the transition we needed.

Until now, observation primarily meant:

**I can see what is happening.**

Now it begins to mean:

**I can see what is happening well enough to work with it.**

A person who sees only:

_I am angry_

has little structural information.

A person who can see:

*a memory is being recalled;

attention is narrowing around it;

a particular interpretation is being reinforced;

the body is becoming tense;

an expectation is forming;

and the whole configuration is producing an impulse toward confrontation*

has something much more useful.

The state has become legible.

And what is legible can become available for deliberate work.

Deliberate work does not mean unlimited control.

It means that the operation is conscious rather than merely automatic.

Now step back.

We have been using an emotional state as our example.

But the principle is larger.

A state is one kind of organization.

A belief is another.

A worldview is another.

A philosophical theory is another.

A scientific model is another.

A religious interpretation is another.

A behavioral strategy is another.

These may differ enormously in content.

But they can all become objects of examination.

They can be entered.

Observed.

Compared.

Modified.

Rejected.

Reconstructed.

You do not have to destroy a model in order to understand it.

You do not have to worship a framework in order to use it.

You do not have to become identical with a worldview in order to think through it.

You can stand in relation to a construction.

Enter it deliberately.

Use it for a particular purpose.

Leave it.

Compare it with another construction.

Return to it from another position.

This is the beginning of the meta-systemic attitude.

And it is exactly the attitude we are introducing at the psychological level.

The state is not an enemy.

It is not a master.

It is not automatically the self.

It is a configuration.

And a configuration can become an object of examination.

Now perform the final test.

Wait for the next state that becomes noticeable.

When it appears, do not immediately ask:

**How do I get rid of this?**

Ask instead:

**What is this made of?**

**What is it doing to the field of attention?**

**What does it make available through Recall?**

**What does it make less available through Differentiation?**

**What direction is it preparing?**

And finally:

**If this configuration were available for examination, what could I change?**

You may discover that some parts are easier to alter than others.

Some may be immediately accessible.

Some may require practice.

This is information about the structure.

Observe it.

The purpose is not to become omnipotent over experience.

The purpose is to stop treating every configuration as though it were an unquestionable command.

A state can be intense without being absolute.

It can have a strong organizing tendency

without determining every subsequent configuration.

It can influence attention

without making every subsequent movement identical with itself.

It can recruit memory

without exhausting the possibilities available through memory.

It can produce an impulse

without making that impulse an obligation.

It can become associated with identity

without thereby becoming identical with the self.

And most importantly:

**it can become visible as a configuration.**

Once visible,

it can be examined.

Once examined,

its elements can be distinguished.

Once its elements are distinguished,

their relations can be questioned.

Once their relations can be questioned,

alternative configurations can be considered.

Once an alternative configuration is deliberately selected

and the corresponding operation is performed,

the configuration can be restructured.

That is the movement we have been preparing for.

We are not merely learning to stand outside experience.

We are learning to **work with what appears**.

The observer is not valuable because it escapes the field.

The observer is valuable because it allows the field to become visible as a field.

Once the configuration is visible, you no longer have to encounter it only from inside itself.

You can examine it.

Compare it.

Identify its components.

Question its relations.

And discover what becomes possible

when its organization is treated as something that can be investigated.

That is the real purpose of observation.

Not distance for its own sake.

**Visibility that makes deliberate operation possible.**

And this leads directly to the next question.

If a state can be examined as a configuration,

can one configuration be deliberately replaced by another?

Can previously formed organization be recalled, selected, and incorporated into a new configuration?

Can attention be deliberately moved from one available organization toward another?

In other words:

**Can we actually rearrange the construction?**
