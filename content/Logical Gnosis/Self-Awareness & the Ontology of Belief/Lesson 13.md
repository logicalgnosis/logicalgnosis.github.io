___
## How Does Experience Become “Me” or “Mine”?

___
## Lesson 13

We ended the last lesson with a question.

What happens when the moving mind does not merely continue,

but begins to claim what it is experiencing as _mine_?

Let's not rush to answer.

Try something simple first.

Bring to mind a mild irritation.

Something small.

Perhaps a slight impatience with the pace of this lesson.

Notice the irritation itself.

Whatever tension is present.

Whatever sensation accompanies it.

Whatever movement is happening in the mind.

Now say two sentences to yourself,

slowly.

_Irritation is present._

Then:

_I am irritated._

Notice if they land differently.

Ask yourself:

**What actually changed between those two sentences?**

The irritation itself may be exactly the same.

Same sensation.

Same intensity.

Same situation.

And yet the second sentence can feel heavier.

More total.

As if the irritation has stopped being something that is happening

and has become something you are.

That difference is what we need to examine.

In the first sentence,

irritation is content.

Something appearing within experience,

the way a sound appears,

the way a memory appears,

the way a thought appears.

In the second,

something else has happened.

The irritation has been related to the self.

It has become:

_my irritation._

Or:

_me being irritated._

A relationship has changed.

The experience has been appropriated.

This is the function we previously called **Ahamkara**.

A function through which experience is organized in relation to _I_ and _mine_.

Look at what we have already mapped.

Previously formed material can become available again.

Mental activity can move through what becomes available.

A state can gather from several interacting elements.

And the same experience can then be organized in relation to the self.

The state becomes:

_This is happening to me._

That is Ahamkara.

Notice what Ahamkara adds to the organization of experience.

Manas moves through the event.

It can connect one thought with another.

It can follow an association.

It can move from one image to another.

It can shift from one thought to another.

But Ahamkara performs a more specific operation.

It organizes the event in relation to the self.

It takes something occurring within the field

and organizes it as belonging to the one experiencing it.

That is **appropriation**.

The claiming of experience as _I_ or _mine_.

This is **Differentiation** at the level of self-reference.

The experience is distinguished

in relation to what is _me_

and what is _not-me_,

what is _mine_

and what is _not-mine_.

Now notice something important.

This claiming usually does not announce itself.

You do not normally think:

_I will now appropriate this irritation as part of my identity._

The appropriation is already happening

by the time you notice its result.

Just as the organizing processes we examined earlier

often become visible only after they have already begun,

Ahamkara can operate beneath ordinary attention.

The irritation appears.

The mind moves through it.

And almost immediately:

_I am irritated._

The state has acquired an owner.

Now watch what happens next.

Suppose you say:

_I am irritated._

What tends to follow?

Perhaps:

_Why am I always like this?_

_People always make me feel this way._

_I am becoming an angry person._

Notice how quickly a temporary state

can become evidence for a much larger claim.

A few seconds of irritation

become a statement about a person.

Then the statement can become part of a continuing self-description.

_I am irritated right now._

becomes:

_I am someone who gets irritated easily._

And eventually:

_This is just who I am._

Ask yourself:

**Did the irritation itself make that claim?**

Or did something else make the claim

using the irritation as material?

This reveals something important.

Ahamkara can appropriate an experience

and organize it in relation to an emerging self-description.

If the same configuration recurs,

that self-description can become increasingly familiar,

stable,

and difficult to distinguish from the person who experiences it.

This is where recurrence becomes important.

A configuration that is repeatedly re-entered

can become stabilized.

A stabilized configuration can function as a belief—

including a belief about oneself.

Notice the distinction.

Ahamkara performs the appropriation.

When the same configuration appears again and again,

the self-referential pattern can become increasingly familiar

and stable.

It can eventually become something like:

_I am this kind of person._

This is one reason identification can become so difficult to notice.

The identity that results

can later become part of the very framework

through which new experience is organized.

But pause here.

The point is not to declare every sense of self false.

Nor is Ahamkara simply an error.

Appropriation has a real functional role in ordinary experience.

You can say:

_I am tired._

_I should rest._

_I am responsible for this._

_I care about this person._

These are not necessarily problems.

The question is narrower.

And more precise.

**Is every appropriation accurate?**

Does every state justify the identity that is built around it?

Does a passing emotion prove something permanent about the person experiencing it?

Does one failure establish:

_I am a failure_?

Does one moment of fear establish:

_I am a fearful person_?

Does one desire establish:

_This is what I truly am_?

Test this directly.

Recall a state from your recent past.

Something you were once convinced said something fundamental about you.

Perhaps a mood.

A failure.

A moment of anger.

A period of anxiety.

Something that, while it was happening,

felt like:

_This is who I am._

Now ask yourself:

**Where is that state now?**

The irritation may have disappeared.

The anxiety may have lifted.

The anger may have cooled.

The confidence may have changed.

The desire may no longer be present.

And yet you can still remember:

_I was angry._

_I was anxious._

_I felt defeated._

Notice what this language reveals.

The state was part of your experience.

But the state itself did not remain identical with you.

Something that once seemed to define you

has changed.

A state can change

while the fact that you experienced that state

remains intelligible.

The claim:

_This state is me_

therefore does more than describe the state.

It performs an additional operation.

**Appropriation.**

Now return to the two sentences.

_Irritation is present._

_I am irritated._

Notice that the first does not deny the experience.

It does not suppress it.

It does not pretend to be calm.

It simply describes what is occurring.

The second adds a relation to identity.

And once you can notice that relation,

a new distinction becomes available.

**The appearance of an experience**

is one thing.

**The appropriation of that experience as “me” or “mine”**

is another.

This is the distinction we are beginning to practice.

Try it again with something else.

Notice a thought.

Before:

_This is what I think._

Simply notice:

_A thought has appeared._

Notice an emotion.

Before:

_This is how I am._

Notice:

_An emotional state is present._

Notice a desire.

Before:

_I need this._

Notice:

_A desire has appeared._

You are not denying the thought.

You are not denying the emotion.

You are not pretending the desire does not matter.

You are observing the movement by which an experience becomes personal.

And now something even more important becomes possible.

That movement itself can become an object of observation.

You can notice:

_This thought is being claimed as mine._

_This emotion is being claimed as me._

_This state is being used as evidence about who I am._

This is not merely the observation of an experience.

It is the observation of the **operation through which experience becomes self-referential**.

The state can be observed.

The appropriation can be observed.

And the relation between them can be distinguished.

That is a significant increase in structural resolution.

You are no longer observing only _what_ appears.

You are beginning to observe **what the mind does with what appears**.

You do not need to decide immediately whether appropriation is good or bad.

You do not need to suppress it.

You do not need to construct an ideal self that never appropriates anything.

The first achievement is more fundamental.

You can **see the operation itself**.

And once appropriation becomes visible,

it is no longer completely unnoticed.

Identification may still happen.

The state may still feel deeply personal.

The thought may still feel convincing.

But you are no longer seeing only the state.

You are also seeing the operation

through which the state becomes _mine_.

This is the point at which a previously compressed process

becomes structurally distinguishable.

Experience.

Appropriation.

Self-reference.

Observation.

Each can now be distinguished

without pretending that they are separate entities.

And that opens the next question.

If a state can appear,

and if its appropriation can also be observed,

what would it mean to observe the state fully

without immediately becoming identical with it?

That is where we go next.
